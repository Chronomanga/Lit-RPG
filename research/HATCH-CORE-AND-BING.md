### H25 — Opening formula

Hatch recommends first establishing a person readers can care about through competence, sympathy, or initiative. Choose the story setup, then shape an opening around disruption, orientation, an ingenuity test, danger followed by the first gain, and commitment to a goal. His literal prescription uses five chapters, avoids fighting before chapter four, and delays the first reward until that chapter. These are a particular template, not a requirement: the functions can overlap in an immediate-danger opening. The claims that a specific chapter creates a chemical bridge, that earlier rewards cannot work, or that this guarantees continued reading are not established by evidence presented here. Keep the useful combination of human investment, understandable choices, earned progress, and forward purpose. A hero can show character while escaping a dungeon; an ordinary-world chapter is optional. No external template, paid course, or linked novel was studied.

### H21 — System and power progression essentials

[Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3nuPVvGhKsk). Hatch favors a powerful low-level protagonist, brisk early advancement, plentiful skills and notifications, rare gifts, exceptional achievement rewards, and playful friction with the System. He highlights aura, meditation, and regeneration as repeated examples. His assignment asks how leveling works, what each level grants, where the first major power spike lands, which rare gift surprises the world, and how the hero relates to the System. The claimed bestseller universality and revenue anecdotes are not independently established. These options suit a generous power fantasy but conflict with sources warning about notification overload and arbitrary exceptions. Our implementation will pick a coherent reward style, show useful changes, and retain costs instead of requiring every listed trope. A legendary class need not grant immediate mastery, healing, or limitless energy. A speaking System is optional; its information and authority still need rules.

### H16 — Magic, identity, and reputation

[Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=yqu3GWPUvFc). Hatch proposes unusual magic, apparently weak powers later revealed as taboo, concealed strength, and impressive specialized knowledge. These patterns can connect power to social reputation and create discoveries beyond a stat increase. He also claims a newcomer surprising experts is widespread among bestsellers and recommends it strongly. The transcript does not provide a reproducible dataset or establish causation. Our version asks where the hero learned a relevant technique, why local experts missed this application, what information the newcomer lacks, and how the discovery changes relationships or authority. Conventional magic can still work. Neither a secret identity nor a weak-to-taboo reveal is mandatory. Expertise should create plausible insight rather than make every local person foolish, and hidden power needs a reason for secrecy and a consequential reveal.

### H10 — Ending and payoff, lesson 9

[Video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZPq19W8Wk3Y). Hatch's final-act template moves from renewed commitment and repairing alliances into escalating confrontation. Earlier skills and information should become useful; the antagonist adapts, and the hero's changed choices help make victory possible. After the climax, show consequences, resolve the book's central arc, and optionally seed the next volume. His fixed chapters 31–40, optional 41, solitary final encounter, villain explanation, relinquished desire, and mirrored ending image are structural options. A revelation should clarify established conflict; it need not be a monologue. A decisive resource or ally should have a prior basis rather than arrive as a rescue invented at the climax. Emotional growth need not always mean giving up the original goal. Preserve the strongest principle: this volume delivers an earned resolution even if the larger story continues.

## Original application decisions

Use functional opening beats rather than fixed chapter placement. Plan advantages and limitations together. Give intellectual victories an information basis. Pair the final use of a power with a character choice. Separate local closure from a new series question. These are editorial design decisions, not a claimed transcript of a single source's method.
