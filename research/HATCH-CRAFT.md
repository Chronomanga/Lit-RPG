## 1. Protagonist patterns

[LitRPG Protagonist Patterns That Actually Sell | (Lesson 1: LitRPG Bestselling Outline Formula)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=l_6SIPfc7zc) — March 27, 2026; 8:51. Local file: `youtube/l_6SIPfc7zc.transcript.txt`; 263 cues; complete coverage 00:00–08:49.

**Capsule:** Hatch describes a commercially oriented protagonist package: male, able to advance independently, unusually powerful, distinct in personality, and willing to help when help also serves a personal goal. His useful planning exercise asks what the character's advantage is, how to describe their personality in one sentence, and what moral reasoning guides their actions. Independence means retaining initiative even with companions; it does not require permanent solitude. He initially says writers need not use every ingredient and acknowledges protagonists of any gender can succeed. Later claims about what most readers want become much stronger. Treat gender, overwhelming strength, and rewarded altruism as his suggested market positioning, not entry requirements or verified audience laws. For the novel plan, retain clear choices, memorable reactions, and a defined moral outlook while deciding separately how much the hero needs other people.

## 2. Opening choices

[Why Most LitRPG Authors Botch Their Opening | The Fix (Lesson 2: LitRPG Bestselling Outline Formula)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvwhtGd4Tq0) — April 3, 2026; 7:45. Local file: `youtube/vvwhtGd4Tq0.transcript.txt`; 229 cues; complete coverage 00:00–07:42.

**Capsule:** Begin with pressure that prompts a revealing choice or reaction, so readers encounter a person before receiving extensive world or system explanations. Provide an early action that earns trust, usually a moral decision. Hatch favors kindness the protagonist can afford rather than either cold refusal or unlimited self-sacrifice. He also proposes confronting a much stronger authority and receiving an unusual connection or ability; the authority can be a boss, ruler, or system rather than a literal deity, and the reward need not come from the figure opposed. His assignment is to sketch those opening beats before drafting chapters. The practical takeaway is a causal opening: disturbance, revealing response, reason to care, and a direction of travel. Defying gods and receiving gifts are optional plot choices, not substitutes for the hero making decisions. His own example includes being defeated and helped by someone else.

Anchors: [00:28 revealing conflict](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvwhtGd4Tq0&t=28s); [01:46 early moral choice](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvwhtGd4Tq0&t=106s); [03:03 unequal authority](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvwhtGd4Tq0&t=183s); [06:18 sketch first](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vvwhtGd4Tq0&t=378s).

## 3. Power and system design

[8 LitRPG Power Mechanics That Make Readers Addicted (Lesson 3: LitRPG Bestselling Outline Formula)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZpdO49AH0fU) — April 16, 2026; 7:23. Local file: `youtube/ZpdO49AH0fU.transcript.txt`; 214 cues; complete coverage 00:00–07:21.

**Capsule:** The eight proposed ingredients are capability beyond apparent level, fast early advancement, abundant system bonuses, unexpectedly rare gains, rapid skill accumulation, extra achievement rewards, friction with a responsive system, and a combination of aura, meditation, and regeneration. Hatch suggests more space between level gains later and making the system feel like a participant through its responses. His strongest planning exercise is concrete: determine how levels work, what each gives, the first major jump in capability, the unusual reward, and the system's relationship with the protagonist. These are useful prompts for a progression plan. His repeated claims that readers always want more notifications or exceptional rewards are positioning advice, not a demonstrated universal preference. The owner's playbook should explain each important power through its use, limits, and costs; these specifics are an adaptation requirement, not a complete balancing method supplied by this video. Aura, meditation, and regeneration remain optional abilities.

Anchors: [00:48 capability and level](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZpdO49AH0fU&t=48s); [01:40 early progression](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZpdO49AH0fU&t=100s); [04:35 responsive system](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZpdO49AH0fU&t=275s); [06:33 planning assignment](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZpdO49AH0fU&t=393s).

## 4. Conflict, companions, and world choices

[Why "Fake" Conflict Sells More LitRPG Books (Lesson 5: LitRPG Bestselling Outline Formula)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VwWU9NkuPgI) — May 2, 2026; 9:45. Local file: `youtube/VwWU9NkuPgI.transcript.txt`; 291 cues; complete coverage 00:00–09:41.

**Capsule:** Hatch proposes apparent handicaps that let a hero demonstrate inventive dominance, a victory over a previously undefeated threat, protective relationships, dangerous animal companions, convenient storage, and bounded challenge spaces such as dungeons or towers. Storage can remove repetitive logistics; companions can create warmth and stakes; a known threat can make a victory legible. His assignment asks how an obstacle displays capability and how a companion supports the protagonist's agency. He also recommends energetic prose, short sentences in tension, and occasional pop-culture references. These are choices to test against the story's voice, not a demand for clipped prose or references in every setting. His preference for superficial lasting damage is a specific low-friction power-fantasy model. It conflicts with his later call for permanent losses. For this owner, use clever workarounds alongside consequences that remain real, and give companions decisions of their own rather than treating them solely as extensions of the hero.

## 5. Act One architecture

[Why Most LitRPG Authors Get These Act One Chapters Wrong (Lesson 6: The LitRPG Bestselling Outline)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BUWE0kq-pWA) — May 22, 2026; 13:42. Local file: `youtube/BUWE0kq-pWA.transcript.txt`; 402 cues; complete coverage 00:00–13:41.

**Capsule:** This introduces a proposed forty-chapter structure and then its first quarter. The sequence establishes a blocked want and deeper need, a reason to trust the hero, a mystery and thematic question, an antagonist connection, worsening pressure, the cost of remaining unchanged, a disruption, its fallout, support or knowledge, resistance, and an irreversible commitment. Hatch uses personal, social, and professional loss as possible stakes alongside physical danger. The useful outlining instruction is modest: a few sentences or a paragraph per proposed chapter, explaining what happens, where, and how the scene connects to the character's goal or flaw. Treat these as scene functions that may combine, move, or expand. Neither ten opening chapters nor a delayed commitment is mandatory. Claims that this architecture applies to every genre and can produce major earnings are assertions. The supplied lesson does not demonstrate a universal forty-scene law or commercial causation.

## 6. First half of the middle

[Why Most LitRPG Authors Lose Readers in the Middle | Lesson 7: LitRPG Bestselling Outline](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=rVdslbTP8sc) — June 5, 2026; 11:21. Local file: `youtube/rVdslbTP8sc.transcript.txt`; 335 cues; complete coverage 00:00–11:20.

**Capsule:** The proposed chapters eleven through twenty move through deliberate entry into an unfamiliar situation, allies and opposition, a relationship that challenges the hero's beliefs, escalating trials, an earned victory, an antagonist's intervention, withheld information, a larger truth and choice, and a midpoint turn. The trials should deliver the experience promised by the premise and produce growth even when the hero loses. A victory can create overconfidence or expose a weakness the opposition later uses. At the midpoint, new information and self-recognition change the goal or the way the hero pursues it. Hatch explicitly revises his initial claim that the hero becomes proactive only here: the character should pursue goals throughout. That correction matters more than the shorthand. Use the outline to connect action, learning, relationships, and opposition; do not postpone agency or the story's main attraction until a prescribed chapter. His percentages and chapter slots are one planning template.

## 7. Second half of the middle

[The Chapters Most LitRPG Authors Are Too Scared to Write | Lesson 8: LitRPG Bestselling Outline](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=LJ5Wn5q7p6o) — June 17, 2026; 7:59. Local file: `youtube/LJ5Wn5q7p6o.transcript.txt`; 248 cues; complete coverage 00:00–07:57.

**Capsule:** Hatch's proposed chapters twenty-one through thirty tighten external pressure, expose the damage caused by the hero's flaw, build a plan with allies, give the hero meaningful responsibility, and let an informed opponent strike a weakness. The plan fails with lasting consequences; a revelation makes the stakes personal; exhaustion and doubt precede renewed perspective from another person. The useful causal chain is choice, responsibility, consequence, reflection, and a better way forward. He stresses that failure does not require a passive hero: the character continues pursuing a goal. An ally's support works through vulnerability and a specific insight, rather than a generic inspirational speech. Keep that emotional precision while choosing a low point appropriate to the novel. Permanent injury, death, total despair, and exactly ten downward chapters are not mandatory. Do not assume every defeat must arise from a personal flaw; a capable opponent, scarce resources, or an impossible tradeoff can also create honest pressure.

## 8. Earlier protagonist checklist

[Your LitRPG Protagonist is Boring (5 Traits Readers Actually Crave)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=3frdVHT6Hac) — February 23, 2026; 8:14. Local file: `youtube/3frdVHT6Hac.transcript.txt`; 255 cues; complete coverage 00:00–08:10.

**Capsule:** The earlier five-part checklist recommends a recognizable personality, useful capability beyond apparent rank, concealed depth revealed at a strong dramatic moment, morally bounded help that also offers a benefit, and narrow knowledge that surprises specialists. Its clearest diagnostic asks whether replacing the protagonist with someone else would change their dialogue and reactions. It also presents strategy and turning disadvantages into advantages as ways to show power, rather than relying entirely on a rare starting class. For the novel plan, establish where expertise came from and let the character demonstrate it against a concrete problem. Avoid making everyone else ignorant merely to manufacture superiority. The video calls its findings data and ties rating count to commercial performance, but its research method, retention measurements, Amazon search claim, income anecdote, and percentage advantage are not demonstrated in the supplied captions. Use the craft prompts without repeating those claims as established facts.

## Comparison and limits for synthesis

| Issue | What these sources actually support | How to handle it in the playbook |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earlier versus later protagonist lists | The February checklist emphasizes hidden depth and specialist knowledge. March Lesson One adds male and independent protagonists while repeating memorable personality, unusual power, and mutual-benefit morality. Different lists are selections, not an explicit retraction. | Use a menu of character choices. Do not merge them into an expanded compulsory test. Compare any separately researched master list against its own wording; it was not reread in this bounded assignment. |
| Power through strategy versus gifts | February stresses strategy instead of a fortunate starting class. April's opening and power lessons advocate divine attention, rare abilities, and unusually generous rewards. | These can be combined if a gift creates a choice and requires learning. The gift alone does not demonstrate earned mastery. |
| Independence versus support | March defines independence strongly, including surviving without the party. April's opening example includes rescue. June gives mentors, allies, and a supporting relationship major roles. | Keep the protagonist responsible for meaningful decisions; allow mutual dependence and competent allies. Agency and doing everything alone are different design choices. |
| Cosmetic obstacles versus lasting costs | May Lesson Five recommends mostly superficial damage and apparent limitations. June Lesson Eight explicitly requests real, permanent consequences and an emotionally credible failure. | Treat these as differing modes or stages of a power fantasy. Decide what failure can cost in the specific novel, then honor that decision. Do not label empty threats as an established recipe for tension. |
| Agency at the midpoint | Lesson Seven starts with a reactive/proactive shorthand, then explicitly corrects it; Lesson Eight reiterates proactive pursuit from the start. | The midpoint changes the goal, understanding, or commitment. It does not grant permission to make the first half passive. |
| Rules versus patterns | Lesson One allows selecting ingredients. Other passages say a particular trope must appear or readers always love it. Forty chapters are presented as a structure, with occasional numbering slips. | Separate source description, author opinion, and chosen project requirements. Use beat functions and test them against the story rather than assigning a universal chapter count. |
| Prose rhythm | Lesson Five favors energetic writing and short sentences in tense passages. It does not provide evidence that every sentence should be short. | Use common words and varied sentences. Let action, attention, and emotion shape rhythm; do not manufacture staccato. |

The research language shifts among top-selling books, highly rated series, rating counts, retention, psychological explanations, and reader search behavior. These measures are not interchangeable. No independent sample audit, comparison group, search dataset, revenue verification, or causal test was performed here. Course invitations, income milestones, and sales promises recur in the transcripts; preserve their promotional context. The notes establish what Hatch recommends, not that following a list produces a bestseller.

For the current project, design and complete the novel first. A later Pocket FM adaptation can reconsider episode boundaries, spoken system passages, and performance. These eight videos do not establish Pocket FM's requirements or prove that a forty-chapter novel should become forty audio episodes. Keep use, limits, costs, choices, and consequences understandable in the novel before choosing an adaptation format.
